Expected counts are comfortably above 5 in every cell, so the chi-squared approximation is valid. The p value is well above 0.05 (≈0.51), so there is no evidence of an association between strain and replicate — consistent with mice being allocated to replicates independently of strain.

Section 2 answer 
cor.test(dosage$initial_weight_g, dosage$weight_lost_g)
cor.test(dosage$tail_length_mm, dosage$initial_weight_g)
cor.test(dosage$tail_length_mm, dosage$initial_weight_g, method = "spearman")

initial_weight_g and weight_lost_g are moderately positively correlated (r ≈ 0.59, p < 0.001) — heavier mice tended to lose more weight. tail_length_mm and initial_weight_g are weakly negatively correlated (r ≈ -0.23, p < 0.001). Both conclusions hold under a Spearman correlation as well.

initial_weight_g is non-normal in both groups (Shapiro-Wilk p < 0.001), so a Wilcoxon test is the safer choice; it agrees with a t-test here (p < 0.001 either way) — males are heavier at baseline. Tail length differs strongly by strain (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.001); the boxplot shows Black6 mice have noticeably shorter tails than BALBc or CD1.

Section 4 answer 
dosage %>% lm_test(initial_weight_g ~ sex)
dosage %>% lm_test(tail_length_mm ~ drug_dose_g)
dosage %>% summarise(mean_dose = mean(drug_dose_g), mean_tail = mean(tail_length_mm), .by = mouse_strain)
dosage %>% lm_test(tail_length_mm ~ drug_dose_g + mouse_strain)

The lm_test(initial_weight_g ~ sex) statistic and p value match the t-test exactly, confirming the equivalence.

This is a good example of Simpson’s paradox: the uncorrected slope for drug_dose_g is strongly negative (higher dose associated with shorter tails), but Black6 mice happen to have both a higher average dose and much shorter tails than the other strains. Once mouse_strain is added to the model, the drug_dose_g slope flips to strongly positive — within any given strain, higher doses are actually associated with longer tails. The uncorrected relationship was driven entirely by strain differences, not a true dose effect in the opposite direction. This is worth discussing explicitly: correcting for a confounder can change not just the strength but the direction of an estimated effect.

The cross-tab reveals perfect separation: every single Black6 mouse falls in the short-tail group (0 long-tailed Black6 mice out of 119). Including mouse_strain as a predictor of long_tail would make the model unstable (infinite/unreliable coefficient for Black6), so it must be left out of this particular model.

Both initial_weight_z and sex are significant predictors: each 1-SD increase in starting weight is associated with roughly a 59% decrease in the odds of having a long tail (OR ≈ 0.41), while male mice have roughly 6-fold higher odds of a long tail than female mice (OR ≈ 6.2), holding starting weight constant.
